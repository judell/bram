  Pedals Through Time: A History of the Bicycle

  The bicycle occupies a curious place in modern life. It is simultaneously a child’s toy, a serious athlete’s
  instrument, a working person’s commute, and a symbol of freedom. Yet for all its familiarity, the bicycle is a
  relatively recent invention—one whose story tracks the industrial age, urban growth, and shifting ideas about who
  gets to move freely through the world. From wooden running machines to carbon-fiber racers, the bicycle’s history
  is a history of incremental ingenuity meeting social change.

  Walking Machines and the First Two-Wheelers

  The bicycle’s ancestry is usually traced to 1817, when Karl von Drais, a German inventor, introduced the
  Laufmaschine, or “running machine.” Later nicknamed the draisine or dandy horse, it consisted of two wheels in a
  line, a wooden frame, and a padded saddle. There were no pedals. The rider sat astride and propelled the machine
  by pushing against the ground with their feet, then coasted. Steering came from a front wheel that could be
  turned.

  Drais’s invention was not a frivolous novelty. Crop failures after the eruption of Mount Tambora in 1815 had made
  horses expensive to feed; a human-powered alternative had practical appeal. Draisines appeared in parks and on
  promenades across Europe, especially among fashionable young men. They were also heavy, uncomfortable, and poorly
  suited to rough roads. Public enthusiasm faded within a few years, but the essential idea—two wheels, one behind
  the other, under a seated rider—had been planted.
